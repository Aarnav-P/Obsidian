So the workflow in summary should be:

Track to obtain position and orientation $x(t), \quad y(t), \quad \theta(t).$

 $\theta(t)$ is obtained by tracking two  points, a front dot and a rear dot:
 $$
    \theta(t) =
    \tan^{-1}
    \left(
    \frac{y_{\mathrm{front}}(t) - y_{\mathrm{rear}}(t)}
         {x_{\mathrm{front}}(t) - x_{\mathrm{rear}}(t)}
    \right),
$$
The images process is:
 Correct for distortion → Track particle in pixel space → Homography to real plane converts pixels to coordinates 
\subsection{Unit Orientation Vector}

The heading angle can be represented by a unit vector
$$
    \mathbf{u}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos \theta(t) \\
    \sin \theta(t)
    \end{pmatrix}.
$$

This avoids problems with the periodicity of the angle.

\subsection{Orientation Autocorrelation Function}

The orientation autocorrelation function is defined as
$$
    C(\tau) =
    \left\langle
    \mathbf{u}(t) \cdot \mathbf{u}(t+\tau)
    \right\rangle,
$$
where $\tau$ is the lag time and the brackets denote an average over all valid
times $t$ and over all trajectories.

Since
$$
    \mathbf{u}(t) \cdot \mathbf{u}(t+\tau)
    =
    \cos\left[\theta(t+\tau) - \theta(t)\right],
$$
the correlation function can be computed as
$$
    C(\tau) =
    \left\langle
    \cos\left[\theta(t+\tau) - \theta(t)\right]
    \right\rangle.
$$

For a video recorded at frame rate $f$, a lag of $k$ frames corresponds to
$$
    \tau = \frac{k}{f}.
$$

Thus, for discrete data,
$$
    C(k) =
    \left\langle
    \cos\left[\theta_{i+k} - \theta_i\right]
    \right\rangle,
$$
where the average is taken over all pairs of points separated by $k$ frames
within the same continuous track.

\subsection{Connection to Persistence Time}

For a simple persistent random walk or active Brownian particle model in two
dimensions, the orientation correlation decays exponentially:
$$
    C(\tau) = \exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\tau_p}\right),
$$
where $\tau_p$ is the persistence time.

In experimental data, it is often useful to fit the more general form
$$
    C(\tau) = A \exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\tau_p}\right),
$$
where $A$ accounts for short-time measurement noise or imperfect orientation
tracking. Ideally, $A \approx 1$.

\subsection{Persistence Length}

If the particle moves with mean speed $\bar{v}$, then the persistence length is
$$
    \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
$$

Thus, the procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Track the object position and orientation in each frame.
    \item Convert tracked points into calibrated world coordinates.
    \item Compute the heading angle $\theta(t)$.
    \item Compute the orientation autocorrelation function $C(\tau)$.
    \item Fit $C(\tau)$ to an exponential decay to obtain $\tau_p$.
    \item Estimate the mean speed $\bar{v}$.
    \item Calculate the persistence length using
    $$
        \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
    $$
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Example Estimate}

If the typical velocity is
$$
    \bar{v} \approx 0.15~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
and the persistence length is expected to be approximately
$$
    \ell_p \approx 1~\mathrm{m},
$$
then the corresponding persistence time is
$$
    \tau_p =
    \frac{\ell_p}{\bar{v}}
    =
    \frac{1~\mathrm{m}}{0.15~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}
    \approx 6.7~\mathrm{s}.
$$

Therefore, one would expect the orientation correlation function to decay over a
timescale of several seconds.

\subsection{Practical Considerations}

The correlation function should only be computed within continuous trajectory
segments. If the object leaves the field of view or tracking fails, the trajectory
should be split into separate tracks. Correlations should not be computed across
gaps.

For a track containing $N$ frames, the number of point pairs available at lag
$k$ is
$$
    N - k.
$$
Therefore, the number of available samples decreases at large lag times. Long
lag times are usually noisier and should be interpreted cautiously.

A practical fitting range should exclude very short lag times dominated by
tracking noise and very long lag times with too few samples. For example, if the
expected persistence time is several seconds, one might fit over a range such as
$$
    0.3~\mathrm{s} \lesssim \tau \lesssim 5~\mathrm{s},
$$
or longer if sufficient long trajectories are available.

\subsection{Alternative: Correlation as a Function of Distance}

If the particle speed varies significantly, it may be preferable to compute the
correlation as a function of distance travelled rather than time. In that case,
one computes
$$
    C(s) =
    \left\langle
    \mathbf{u}(r) \cdot \mathbf{u}(r+s)
    \right\rangle,
$$
where $s$ is distance travelled along the trajectory. The persistence length
can then be obtained directly from
$$
    C(s) = \exp\left(-\frac{s}{\ell_p}\right).
$$

For approximately constant speed, the time-based and distance-based approaches
are related by
$$
    s \approx \bar{v}\tau,
$$
so that
$$
    \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
$$

\subsection{Body Orientation vs Velocity Direction}

There are two possible choices for the direction vector.

The first is the body orientation,
$$
    \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{body}}(t)
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos \theta_{\mathrm{body}}(t) \\
    \sin \theta_{\mathrm{body}}(t)
    \end{pmatrix},
$$
where $\theta_{\mathrm{body}}$ is determined from the orientation of the
tracked markers on the object.

The second is the velocity direction,
$$
    \mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{vel}}(t)
    =
    \frac{\mathbf{v}(t)}{|\mathbf{v}(t)|}.
$$

The body orientation measures how long the object's axis remains aligned, while
the velocity direction measures how long the actual trajectory direction remains
persistent. For an ideal self-propelled particle these may be similar, but for a
Hexbug they may differ due to slipping, vibration, collisions, or sideways
motion. In practice, it can be useful to compute both and compare them.
