##### **Notes from meeting:**

- Raushan raises a good point that there may be different mechanisms for movement, e.g. a jitter that takes a Gaussian distribution and a "skip" that has a different angular distribution
	 ∴ it seems more sensible to use the standard correlation function for the persistence length instead of Eloi's method with the diffusion coefficient of a persistent random walk 

So the workflow in summary should be:

Track to obtain position and orientation $x(t), \quad y(t), \quad \theta(t).$

 $\theta(t)$ is obtained by tracking two  points, a front dot and a rear dot:
 $$
    \theta(t) =
    \tan^{-1}
    \left(
    \frac{y_{\mathrm{front}}(t) - y_{\mathrm{rear}}(t)}
         {x_{\mathrm{front}}(t) - x_{\mathrm{rear}}(t)}
    \right),
$$
The images process is:
    _Correct for distortion → Track particle in pixel space → Homography to real plane converts pixels to coordinates → Real space trajectory is used to calculate $\theta(t)$._
 
We then can use the velocity vector $$
    \mathbf{u}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos \theta(t) \\
    \sin \theta(t)
    \end{pmatrix}.
$$
and define the correlation function 
$$
    C(t) =
    \left\langle
    \mathbf{u}(t) \cdot \mathbf{u}(0)
    \right\rangle,
$$
where $t$ is time and the angle brackets denote an average over all valid
times $t$ and over all trajectories.

Since
$$
    \mathbf{u}(t) \cdot \mathbf{u}(t+\tau)
    =
    \cos\left[\theta(t+\tau) - \theta(t)\right],
$$
(simple double-angle formula) the correlation function can be computed as
$$
    C(t) =
    \left\langle
    \cos\left[\theta(t+\tau) - \theta(t)\right]
    \right\rangle.
$$

For a video recorded at frame rate $f$, a time-step of $k$ frames corresponds to
$$
    \tau = \frac{k}{f}.
$$

Thus, for discrete data,
$$
    C(k) =
    \left\langle
    \cos\left[\theta_{i+k} - \theta_i\right]
    \right\rangle,
$$
where the average is taken over all pairs of points separated by $k$ frames within the same continuous track. So between frames $n+k$ and $n$ for $\sum_{n=1}^{N-k}$ for a video with $N$ frames (first frame being frame 1). 

**Equation 1:**
	$$
	C(k)=\dfrac{1}{N-k}\sum_{n=1}^{N-k}\cos\left[\theta_{n+k} - \theta_n\right]
	$$^eq1

For an active Brownian particle (in 2D?), the orientation correlation decays exponentially:
$$
    C(\tau) = \exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\tau_p}\right),
$$
where $\tau_p$ is the persistence time. Even when we do not know if the particle has Gaussian statistics we can define $\tau_p$ as the time at which $C(\tau_p) = \dfrac{1}{e}C(0)$ (noting that $C(0) =1$ by definition).

\subsection{Persistence Length}

If the particle moves with mean speed $\bar{v}$, then the persistence length is
$$
    \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
$$
So to recap:
    - Track the object position and orientation in each frame.
    - Convert tracked points into calibrated world coordinates.
    - Compute the heading angle $\theta(t)$.
    - Compute the orientation autocorrelation function $C(\tau)$.
    - Fit $C(\tau)$ to an exponential decay to obtain $\tau_p$.
    - Calculate from trajectory avg. speed $\bar{v}$.
    - Calculate the persistence length using
    $$
        \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
    $$

The correlation function should really only be computed within continuous trajectory
segments. If the object leaves the FOV the trajectory should be split into separate tracks. 

For a track containing $N$ frames, the number of point pairs available at lag
$k$ is
$$
    N - k.
$$
Therefore, the number of available samples decreases at large lag times. Long
lag times are usually noisier and should be interpreted cautiously.

Practically speaking very short time-steps may be affected by poor tracking causing noise and very long time-steps will have too few samples. Worth experimenting a little with to see how $\tau_p$ changes with choice of $k$.

If the particle speed varies significantly, it may be preferable to compute the
correlation as a function of distance travelled rather than time. In that case,
one computes
$$
    C(s) =
    \left\langle
    \mathbf{u}(r) \cdot \mathbf{u}(r+s)
    \right\rangle,
$$
where $s$ is distance travelled along the trajectory. The persistence length
can then be obtained directly from
$$
    C(s) = \exp\left(-\frac{s}{\ell_p}\right).
$$

For approximately constant speed, the time-based and distance-based approaches
are related by
$$
    s \approx \bar{v}\tau,
$$
so that
$$
    \ell_p = \bar{v}\tau_p.
$$
